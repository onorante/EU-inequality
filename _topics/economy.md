in_short: |
  Policymakers use economic models to anticipate the effects of their decisions, for example a change in interest rates or a public spending programme. For a long time, these models represented the whole economy as if it contained one average household. This made them manageable, but blind to inequality.

  It also made them less accurate. Households with few savings spend almost every extra euro they receive, while wealthier households save a larger part. Who receives support therefore affects how much the whole economy grows, and who is hit by a shock affects how deep a recession becomes. A new generation of models, known as heterogeneous-agent models, represents thousands of different households. This topic develops such models for Europe.

example:

    The average effect may look moderate, but behind it there are winners and losers, and how each group reacts determines how quickly inflation falls and how deep the slowdown is.

## Why it matters for Europe

In the euro area, one monetary policy applies to many countries whose households differ widely in income, wealth, debt and home ownership, while taxes and social transfers remain mainly national. Understanding how policies spread through different households, and across countries, is central to the design of both monetary and fiscal policy in Europe.

## What we study

- **Monetary policy and inequality:** how interest rate decisions redistribute income and wealth, and how this feeds back into the economy.
- **Fiscal policy:** who benefits from taxes, transfers and public investment, and what this means for growth.
- **National redistribution and European risk sharing:** models of two linked economies sharing a currency, to study how much support should come from national policy and how much from sharing risks between countries.
- **Generations and the life cycle:** how young, working-age and retired households are affected differently.
- **Grounding models in data:** using European household data so that models reflect real households.

## Methods and data

We develop heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian (HANK) and overlapping-generations (OLG) models, calibrated on European household microdata such as the Household Finance and Consumption Survey. Where possible, models and code are made openly available.
