  Income is the most common yardstick of inequality, because it is measured regularly and is easy to compare. Yet two households with the same income can live very different lives.

  The cost and quality of housing, access to health care and education, energy bills, the time spent commuting or caring for others, and the opportunities available nearby all shape what people can actually do with their income. This topic develops ways to measure these dimensions, one by one and together, and to see where and for whom they accumulate.

    Anna and Marco have the same net income. Anna owns a well-insulated flat in a city, a ten-minute walk from a train station, a school and a hospital.     Marco spends a larger share of his income on heating and fuel and more of his time travelling, and he has fewer job opportunities nearby. In income statistics, Anna and Marco are equal. In everyday life, they are not.

## Why it matters for Europe

EU policies on social rights, cohesion, the energy transition and digital inclusion need indicators that show which groups and which areas are left behind, and on which dimension. Without them, support risks being spread too thinly or reaching the wrong people. Measures at regional and local level allow policies to be targeted where needs are greatest.

## What we study

- **Income, as the baseline:** earnings, transfers and taxes, and how the other dimensions add to income gaps or offset them.
- **Housing:** affordability, quality, overcrowding, and the divide between owners and renters.
- **Health:** access to care, and differences in health outcomes between groups and places.
- **Education and skills:** unequal access to education and to learning throughout life.
- **Childhood and generations:** how the circumstances children grow up in shape their chances later in life, how disadvantage passes from parents to children, and the gaps between young and old.
- **Digital access:** connectivity and digital skills, increasingly a condition for work and for public services.
- **Energy and environment:** energy poverty, exposure to pollution and climate risks, and differences in households' carbon footprints.
- **Access to services and transport:** distance and travel time to schools, hospitals, shops and public offices.
- **Time poverty:** people who lack free time because of long working hours, commuting or care duties.
- **Subjective well-being:** how people themselves assess their lives and their prospects.
- **Territorial gaps:** differences between regions, between cities and rural areas, and between neighbourhoods of the same city.

## Methods and data

We build indices that combine several dimensions into a single picture while keeping each dimension visible, indicators that describe whole distributions rather than averages, and measures that follow people over their life course.

We give preference to data that are geolocated, describe whole distributions, cover more than one dimension, follow people or places over time, and fill gaps in existing statistics: administrative registers, surveys, geographic data and text.
