  Economic news is mostly about averages: average income, average inflation, average growth. Averages are useful, but they can hide what matters most. If average income rises by 2%, everyone may be 2% better off; or some may gain a lot while others lose. To understand inequality, we need to follow the whole distribution.

  This raises two challenges. Economic shocks often hit the extremes of the distribution much harder than the middle, and their effects are not proportional: a large shock can do far more than twice the damage of a small one. ## Why it matters for Europe

During the pandemic, the energy crisis and the recent surge in inflation, policymakers needed to know quickly which households were most exposed. Methods that follow the whole distribution, and that can be updated with timely data, allow earlier and better-targeted responses, and a more accurate assessment of their effects afterwards.

## What we study

- **Shocks and distributions:** how economic shocks (prices, interest rates, recessions) change the entire distribution of income and wealth over time.
- **Extremes and non-linear effects:** why the tails of the distribution react differently, and why large shocks are not simply bigger versions of small ones.
- **Making the most of imperfect data:** combining sources with different frequencies, filling gaps, correcting errors, and estimating distributions early, before official data are released.
- **Climate and the income distribution:** how climate events and the green transition affect households differently.
- **Expectations and uncertainty:** what households expect about prices, jobs and income, and how these expectations shape their decisions.

## Methods and data

We use models in which a whole distribution, rather than a single number, is the object of analysis: distributional and functional time-series models, quantile methods, non-linear and Bayesian models, and machine learning for imputation and harmonisation.

Data include household surveys such as EU-SILC and the Household Finance and Consumption Survey, consumer expectations surveys, administrative records and high-frequency indicators.
